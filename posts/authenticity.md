# Thoughts on 'Authenticity' in Food Culture as an Indian American

**Date:** July 2026


The term 'authentic' over the course of the last decade has garnered a lot of attention and been rewarded, in my opinion, unfairly. This is not to say that certain resteraunts or foods marketing themselves as 'authentic' are not deserving of success. Rather, the term authenticity is often being applied as a verification for merit or quality. 

Authenticity is formally defined by Merriam Webster as the quality of being *authoratative, valid, true, real, or genuine*. Though these days I have been increasingly aware of the subjectivity in anything and everything, for the sake of this argument, let's take this definition to be objective. What then, are examples of foods that satisfy these requirements?

Formal culinary school teaching French cooking techqniques in an assessable manner is certainly *authoratative*. The guy working until 3 am to run his halal cart and sell affordable meals is definitely *real*. The young teenager on Tiktok is being *genuine* when showing how her interracial parents eat family dinner consisting of paneer quesadillas and butter chicken tacos. Personally, mixing payasam with boba *truely* represents the first 18 years of my life, growing up in the South Bay Area, surrounded predominantly by many different Asian immigrants. 

Yet, we as an online society are quick to assign contradictary labels to these foods. Traditional French cooking is insultingly 'unseasoned' and 'basic'; halal cart food is a 'struggle meal'; butter chicken tacos are blasphemy and downright disrespectful to Indian and Latin cuisine.

Of course, individuals are entitled to feel offended by anything they want, but when individuals are not aware of the power of the online hive mentality, this can very quickly be the reason that a small business starts to struggle overnight. 

### As an Indian American

To be authentic can also be defined as being 'made or done the same way as the original'. The original is not stated in the definition, as it is supposed to be contextual. Despite this, authenticity is usually implicitly associated with a general context, one that emphasizes homogenous cultures.  

As a second generation Indian American, I often times experience situations in which people call the food and or resteraunts I grew up on as inauthentic[^1]. I know, of course, that they are referring to authenticity in regards to being Indian. However, India is an incredibly diverse country in terms of its food, changing as you move across the country, between religions, and amongst social classes. Even rice, a grain that is staple throughout all of Asia, has over 50 different variations throughout the nation. 

Somehow, we as an online society decide where to draw a line on how far back we look to differentiate tradition from innovation. The former is associated with culture and heritage, and the latter is not considered evolving culture, but rather, straying away from the roots of your identity -- an identity that user1341 decides for you. I myslef have previously propogated this belief: I chose to refrain from drinking chai at modern coffee shops citing that the addition of cinnamon was nonsensical. At the same time, I certainly didn't drink tea the way it was first drunk in China in the 3rd century. 

This feels unique to the Indian American experience. In India, when you grow up sorrounded by heterogenity, where everyone speaks the same language as you in the state that you are raised in, there is no need to doubt your culture (granted, there are different granularities to this in terms of social class, income, religion, etc. ) [^2]. However, when you live as an Indian American in America, it is very likely that your neighbor does not speak the same language as you nor has the same cultural practices as you. Regardless, they are just as Indian as you. 

To question either one's authenticty to being Indian is fruitless, since no subcategory within in India takes predominance over another in the global setting[^3]. This futher highlights that authenticity relative to being Indian itself is not a meaningful term. 

### Descriptive, not Prescriptive

As global foods became increasingly more accessible, what initially came out as a term meant to provide a **description** of a food has rapidly transformed into a **prescription** of how foods *should* be, thereby counterintuitively limiting the exposure to more foods. 

I often wonder, when people consider the small business that my family frequents to be 'inauthentic', do they see the way it is genuinely, truely and honestly Indian American? It takes a careful eye, one of a regular customer, to look past the pickup counter and see the 3 generations of Punjabi women working tirelessly in a small kitchen to serve customers daily. Although you can hear songs in just about every Indian language playing on the TV, you see signs written in only English. Amongst all the flyers on the door outside, every other one is promoting Shen Yun, which is certainly not Indian. 

Technically, this resteraunt is not authentic to my culture, given that my parents are ethnically Malayali and Tamil. However, everytime my family visited after an arangetram or SAT test, this resteraunt saw me grow up and experience more of my Indian American culture than anyone in Kerala ever did.
